# <span class="chapnum">Chapter 7</span>Instrumentation Amplifier Architectures

The instrumentation amplifier is where the common-mode rejection of the whole chain is won or lost.

## <span class="secnum">7.1</span>The three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier

Gain in the first stage, CMRR set by resistor matching in the difference amplifier, and how the first stage, with a common-mode gain of one, multiplies the second stage's CMRR by its own gain: 0.1 % resistors give a unity-gain difference amplifier only about 54 dB, and a first-stage gain of 100 lifts that to 94 dB. Then why the classic textbook IA is too power-hungry and offset-limited for a microwatt ExG chip.

## <span class="secnum">7.2</span>Current-feedback (indirect current feedback) IAs

Transconductors convert the input and feedback voltages to currents; CMRR no longer depends on resistor matching; limited input range and linearity.

## <span class="secnum">7.3</span>Capacitively coupled IAs

Input capacitors block electrode DC and set the gain by a capacitor ratio, which matches well on chip. CCIAs dominate modern ExG front-ends and lead to the chopper amplifiers of [Chapter 9]({% link chapters/09_chopper.md %}).

## <span class="secnum">7.4</span>CMRR mechanisms and mismatch

Intrinsic CMRR of the amplifier versus CMRR lost to electrode impedance imbalance ([Chapter 4]({% link chapters/04_interference.md %})); budgeting the two against the 89 dB the ECG standards imply. The worst-case CMRR of a difference amplifier with resistor tolerance $$t$$, about $$(1+G)/(4t)$$ {% include cite.html keys="pallas1991" %}, combined with the op-amp's own CMRR; capacitor mismatch plays the same part in a CCIA. CMRR falls with frequency, through mismatched parasitic capacitances and falling loop gain, so it is checked at 50 and 60 Hz and their harmonics, not at DC.

## <span class="secnum">7.5</span>Common-mode range at 1.2 V

Input stages that tolerate the body common-mode voltage and the ±300 mV electrode offset on a low supply.
